Learning Outcomes

By the end of this lecture, you should be able to:

  • Compute double integrals on rectangles, Type I regions, and Type II regions.
  • Change integration order by describing the same region with new slices.
  • Set up triple integrals using Cartesian, cylindrical, and spherical coordinates.
  • Use the Jacobian determinant in general changes of variables.
  • Apply multivariable integrals to area, volume, mass, center of mass, moment of inertia, probability, and average value.

Central Theme

An integral is a sum of density times small size. Lecture 9 organizes how to compute those sums over planar regions, solids, transformed coordinates, and higher-dimensional domains.

Double Integrals

For $f$ on a planar region $D$, $\iint_D f\,dA$ accumulates values over small area elements. If $f=1$, the integral is area. If $f\ge0$, it is the volume below $z=f(x,y)$ over $D$.

Type I and Type II descriptions are slicing choices: vertical slices use $g_1(x)\le y\le g_2(x)$; horizontal slices use $h_1(y)\le x\le h_2(y)$.

Triple Integrals and Solids

A triple integral $\iiint_E f\,dV$ accumulates density through a solid. Common descriptions include boxes, vertical solids, cross-sections, and solids whose symmetry suggests cylindrical or spherical coordinates.

Coordinate Systems

  • Polar: $dA=r\,dr\,d\theta$.
  • Cylindrical: $dV=r\,dr\,d\theta\,dz$.
  • Spherical: $dV=\rho^2\sin\phi\,d\rho\,d\phi\,d\theta$.

The coordinate choice should make the region and integrand simpler, not merely different.

General Change of Variables

For a smooth one-to-one transformation $x=T(u)$, the local volume scaling is $|\det DT(u)|$:

\[ \int_{T(A)} f(x)\,dx = \int_A f(T(u))|\det DT(u)|\,du. \]

Applications

The same formalism computes geometric and physical quantities: area, volume, average value, mass, center of mass, moment of inertia, and probability. The setup is usually the hard part; computation comes after the domain is clear.
