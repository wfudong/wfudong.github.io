Learning Outcomes

By the end of this lecture bundle, you should be able to:

  • Review the main computational language from Lectures 1-6: vectors, geometry, limits, derivatives, gradients, Jacobians, extrema, and Taylor expansion.
  • Interpret double and triple integrals as accumulated total, area, volume, mass, and average value.
  • Set up iterated integrals over rectangles, Type I and Type II regions, vertical solids, and sliced solids.
  • Change the order of integration when a different slicing direction simplifies the computation.
  • Choose Cartesian, polar, cylindrical, or spherical coordinates from the symmetry of the region and integrand.
  • Use the Jacobian determinant as the local scaling factor in a change of variables.

Central Theme

The course now shifts from local behavior to accumulated quantities. Derivatives describe nearby change; integrals collect those local contributions over a whole region.

Review Bridge

The review block ties earlier material to integration:

  • Determinants from linear algebra measure area and volume scaling.
  • Parametrizations describe curves, surfaces, and regions that later become integration domains.
  • Continuity gives a clean sufficient condition for integrability.
  • Jacobians from the chain rule become the scaling factor in coordinate changes.
  • Extrema help estimate integrals and bound accumulated quantities.

Double Integrals

A double integral \(\iint_D f(x,y)\,dA\) accumulates a density \(f\) over a planar region \(D\). On a rectangle, Fubini's theorem turns the accumulation into iterated one-variable integrals:

\[ \iint_{[a,b]\times[c,d]} f(x,y)\,dA = \int_a^b\int_c^d f(x,y)\,dy\,dx. \]

For non-rectangular regions, the main task is choosing bounds. A Type I region uses \(a\le x\le b\), \(g_1(x)\le y\le g_2(x)\); a Type II region uses \(c\le y\le d\), \(h_1(y)\le x\le h_2(y)\).

Practical habit: sketch the region first, label the outside variable, then write the inner bounds as the slice seen by that outside variable.

Triple Integrals

A triple integral \(\iiint_E f(x,y,z)\,dV\) accumulates density through a solid \(E\). Common descriptions include boxes, vertical solids, horizontal slices, and coordinate systems adapted to symmetry.

  • Vertical solids: describe a base region \(D\) in the \(xy\)-plane and bounds \(u(x,y)\le z\le v(x,y)\).
  • Horizontal slices: fix \(z\), integrate over a cross-section \(D_z\), then integrate the result over \(z\).
  • Other slicing directions: use the variable that gives the simplest cross-sections and bounds.

Coordinate Changes

Coordinate changes replace a difficult region by a simpler parameter region, while the Jacobian corrects the area or volume element.

  • Polar coordinates: \(x=r\cos\theta,\ y=r\sin\theta,\ dA=r\,dr\,d\theta\).
  • Cylindrical coordinates: \(x=r\cos\theta,\ y=r\sin\theta,\ z=z,\ dV=r\,dr\,d\theta\,dz\).
  • Spherical coordinates: \(x=\rho\sin\phi\cos\theta,\ y=\rho\sin\phi\sin\theta,\ z=\rho\cos\phi,\ dV=\rho^2\sin\phi\,d\rho\,d\phi\,d\theta\).

In general, for a smooth one-to-one change of variables \(x=T(u)\), the volume element is multiplied by \(|\det DT(u)|\).

Problem Workflow

  1. Identify whether the problem asks for area, volume, mass, average value, or a transformed integral.
  2. Draw the region or solid and choose a slicing direction.
  3. Write bounds from outside to inside, checking each bound against the picture.
  4. Choose coordinates only when symmetry or bounds become simpler.
  5. Include the correct area or volume element, especially the Jacobian factor.
  6. Compute only after the setup is geometrically clear.
