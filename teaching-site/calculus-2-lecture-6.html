Learning Outcomes

By the end of this lecture, you should be able to:

  • Use the Jacobian as a best linear approximation and apply the matrix chain rule.
  • Write first- and second-order Taylor models for functions of several variables.
  • Check local invertibility with a nonzero Jacobian determinant.
  • Use implicit differentiation formulas such as $y'(x)=-F_x/F_y$.
  • Organize unconstrained and constrained extrema through gradients, Hessians, and Lagrange multipliers.

Central Theme

The Jacobian is the best linear model. Taylor expansion adds the next layers, while inverse and implicit function theorems explain when equations and coordinate maps behave regularly near a point.

Jacobian and Chain Rule Review

For a differentiable map $F:\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}^m$, the Jacobian $J_F(a)$ is the unique linear map satisfying $F(a+h)=F(a)+J_F(a)h+o(\|h\|)$.

The chain rule becomes matrix multiplication: $J_{G\circ F}(a)=J_G(F(a))J_F(a)$. This single formula covers curves, parameterized surfaces, inverse maps, and implicit differentiation.

Taylor Models

Taylor's theorem refines the linear approximation. At a point $a$,

\[ f(a+h)=f(a)+\nabla f(a)\cdot h+\frac12 h^{\mathsf T}H_f(a)h+o(\|h\|^2). \]

At a critical point, the linear term disappears, so the Hessian quadratic form controls the first visible local shape.

Inverse Function Theorem

If $F:\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}^n$ is continuously differentiable and $\det J_F(a)\ne0$, then $F$ has a differentiable local inverse near $a$.

The theorem is local. A map may be locally invertible around a point without being one-to-one on the whole domain.

Implicit Function Theorem

If $F(x,y)=0$ and $F_y(a,b)\ne0$, then near $(a,b)$ the equation can be solved as $y=y(x)$, and

\[ y'(x)=-\frac{F_x}{F_y}. \]

The same idea extends to systems: solve for variables whose Jacobian block is invertible.

Extrema Workflow

  1. Ask whether the domain guarantees existence of absolute extrema.
  2. Find interior candidates from $\nabla f=0$ or nondifferentiability.
  3. Classify local candidates using the Hessian or higher-order Taylor terms.
  4. For constraints, solve $\nabla f=\lambda\nabla g$ together with the constraint.
  5. For closed regions, compare interior, boundary, and corner values.
