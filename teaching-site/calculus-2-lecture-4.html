Learning Outcomes

By the end of this lecture, you will be able to:

  • Compute directional derivatives and interpret them with the gradient.
  • Use tangent-plane linearization and identify normal directions consistently.
  • Apply chain rule in one-parameter and multi-parameter settings.
  • Interpret and compute Jacobian matrices as local linear maps.
  • Classify unconstrained critical points using the second derivative test.
  • Solve constrained extrema problems with Lagrange multipliers.

Directional Derivatives and Gradient

Let \(f:\mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}\), \(a=(a,b)\), and \(u=(u_1,u_2)\) with \(\|u\|=1\). The directional derivative is

\[ D_u f(a)=\lim_{t\to 0}\frac{f(a+tu)-f(a)}{t}. \]

If \(f\) is differentiable at \(a\), then

\[ D_u f(a)=\nabla f(a)\cdot u. \]

  • \(\nabla f(a)\) points to steepest increase.
  • Maximum directional derivative is \(\|\nabla f(a)\|\).
  • Minimum directional derivative is \(-\|\nabla f(a)\|\).
  • If \(u\perp \nabla f(a)\), then \(D_u f(a)=0\).

Pitfall: The direction vector in \(D_u f=\nabla f\cdot u\) must be a unit vector. Normalize first if needed.

Example. For \(f(x,y)=x^2y+y^3\),

\[ \nabla f=(2xy,\,x^2+3y^2),\quad \nabla f(1,1)=(2,4). \]

With \(u=(3/5,4/5)\):

\[ D_u f(1,1)=(2,4)\cdot(3/5,4/5)=22/5. \]

Tangent Plane and Normal Direction

For \(z=f(x,y)\) differentiable at \((a,b)\):

\[ z\approx f(a,b)+f_x(a,b)(x-a)+f_y(a,b)(y-b). \]

For \(F(x,y,z)=f(x,y)-z=0\), a normal vector is

\[ \nabla F(a,b,f(a,b))=(f_x(a,b),f_y(a,b),-1). \]

Both \(n\) and \(-n\) are valid. Choose orientation based on wording such as upward/outward/inward.

Chain Rule in Multivariable Calculus

For a level set/surface \(f=\text{const}\), motion tangent to that level has zero instantaneous change, so \(\nabla f\cdot \gamma'(t)=0\). This is the geometric reason the gradient is normal to level curves/surfaces.

Case A: One Parameter

If \(x=x(t), y=y(t), z=f(x,y)\), then

\[ \frac{dz}{dt}=f_x\frac{dx}{dt}+f_y\frac{dy}{dt}=\nabla f\cdot (x'(t),y'(t)). \]

Example. \(f(x,y)=x^2y\), \(x=t^2\), \(y=e^t\):

\[ \frac{dz}{dt}=(4t^3+t^4)e^t. \]

Case B: Two Parameters

If \(x=x(s,t), y=y(s,t), w=f(x,y)\), then

\[ \frac{\partial w}{\partial s}=f_x\frac{\partial x}{\partial s}+f_y\frac{\partial y}{\partial s}, \qquad \frac{\partial w}{\partial t}=f_x\frac{\partial x}{\partial t}+f_y\frac{\partial y}{\partial t}. \]

Jacobian Matrix and Linearization

For \(F:\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}^m\),

\[ J_F(a)=\left[\frac{\partial F_i}{\partial x_j}(a)\right]_{m\times n}. \]

If \(F\) is differentiable at \(a\),

\[ F(a+h)=F(a)+J_F(a)h+o(\|h\|). \]

Example. \(F(x,y)=(x^2-y,xy)\):

\[ J_F(x,y)=\begin{bmatrix}2x & -1\\ y & x\end{bmatrix}, \qquad J_F(1,2)=\begin{bmatrix}2 & -1\\ 2 & 1\end{bmatrix}. \]

Optimization in Two Variables

Critical points satisfy \(\nabla f(a,b)=(0,0)\) (or first partials fail to exist).

Use

\[ A=f_{xx},\;B=f_{xy},\;C=f_{yy},\;D=AC-B^2. \]

  • If \(D>0\) and \(A>0\): local minimum.
  • If \(D>0\) and \(A<0\): local maximum.
  • If \(D<0\): saddle.
  • If \(D=0\): inconclusive.

Example. \(f(x,y)=x^3-3x+y^2\). Critical points: \((1,0)\), \((-1,0)\). Classification: \((1,0)\) is local minimum, \((-1,0)\) is saddle.

Lagrange Multipliers

To optimize \(f(x,y)\) under \(g(x,y)=c\), solve

\[ \nabla f=\lambda\nabla g,\qquad g(x,y)=c. \]

Example. Optimize \(f(x,y)=xy\) on \(x^2+y^2=1\):

Max value \(1/2\), min value \(-1/2\), attained at \((\pm 1/\sqrt2,\pm 1/\sqrt2)\) with matching signs for max and opposite signs for min.

Mini Workflow

  1. Normalize the direction vector for directional derivatives.
  2. Compute gradient first, then project: \(D_u f=\nabla f\cdot u\).
  3. For normals, compute a valid normal and choose sign by orientation words.
  4. For composites, draw dependence and apply chain rule step by step.
  5. For unconstrained extrema, find critical points then use second derivative test.
  6. For constraints, solve \(\nabla f=\lambda\nabla g\) with constraint equation.

Extra In-Class Example Bank

From the updated Lecture 4 PDF, these board-ready examples are added for short in-class drills.

Example 8: Non-Unit Direction

\(f(x,y)=x^2-xy+2y^2,\ a=(1,-1),\ v=(2,1)\). Normalize \(v\) to \(u=v/\|v\|\), then use \(D_u f=\nabla f\cdot u\).

Result: \(\nabla f(1,-1)=(3,-5)\), \(u=(2/\sqrt5,1/\sqrt5)\), so \(D_u f(1,-1)=1/\sqrt5\).

Example 9: Fastest Increase/Decrease

\(f(x,y)=e^x\cos y\) at \((0,\pi/3)\). Since \(\nabla f=(e^x\cos y,-e^x\sin y)\), we get \(\nabla f=(1/2,-\sqrt3/2)\) there.

Fastest increase direction is \((1/2,-\sqrt3/2)\), maximal rate is \(1\), and fastest decrease direction is its negative.

Example 10: Chain Rule Along a Curve

\(f(x,y)=\ln(1+x^2+y^2),\ x=t^2,\ y=\sin t\). Using \(f_xx'+f_yy'\):

\[ \frac{d}{dt}f(x(t),y(t))=\frac{4t^3+\sin(2t)}{1+t^4+\sin^2 t}. \]

Example 11: Unconstrained Optimization

\(f(x,y)=x^2+xy+y^2-4x\). Critical point is \((8/3,-4/3)\).

With \(f_{xx}=2,\ f_{xy}=1,\ f_{yy}=2\), determinant \(D=3>0\) and \(f_{xx}>0\), so it is a strict local minimum.

Example 12: Lagrange on an Ellipse

Optimize \(f(x,y)=x+y\) subject to \(x^2/4+y^2=1\). Solving \(\nabla f=\lambda\nabla g\) gives candidates

\[ \left(\frac{4}{\sqrt5},\frac{1}{\sqrt5}\right),\quad \left(-\frac{4}{\sqrt5},-\frac{1}{\sqrt5}\right), \]

so \(f_{\max}=\sqrt5,\ f_{\min}=-\sqrt5\).

Simple Derivation: Laplacian in Polar Coordinates (2D)

Using \(x=r\cos\theta,\ y=r\sin\theta\), and \(u(x,y)=U(r,\theta)\), chain rule gives:

\[ u_x=U_r\cos\theta-\frac{1}{r}U_\theta\sin\theta,\qquad u_y=U_r\sin\theta+\frac{1}{r}U_\theta\cos\theta. \]

Applying \(\partial_x,\partial_y\) once more and simplifying yields:

\[ \Delta u=u_{xx}+u_{yy}=U_{rr}+\frac{1}{r}U_r+\frac{1}{r^2}U_{\theta\theta}. \]

If \(u=u(r)\) is radial only, then \(u_{\theta\theta}=0\) and

\[ u_{rr}+\frac{1}{r}u_r=0. \]

Project-Style Problem: Multivariable Calculus in MLE (Least Squares Regression)

From the updated PDF, the lecture now includes a project-style application of chain rule, gradient, and Hessian to linear regression:

  1. Write \(\ell(\beta,\sigma^2)\) for \(y_i=x_i^\top\beta+\varepsilon_i,\ \varepsilon_i\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2)\).
  2. For fixed \(\sigma^2\), show maximizing likelihood in \(\beta\) is minimizing \(J(\beta)=\frac12\|X\beta-y\|_2^2\).
  3. Compute \(\nabla_\beta J(\beta)\) via multivariable chain rule.
  4. Compute Hessian \(\nabla_\beta^2J(\beta)\) and explain convexity.
  5. Derive normal equations \(X^\top X\hat\beta=X^\top y\), and closed form when invertible.
  6. Derive \(\hat\sigma^2_{\mathrm{MLE}}=\frac1n\|y-X\hat\beta\|_2^2\).
  7. Implement gradient descent \(\beta^{(k+1)}=\beta^{(k)}-\eta\nabla_\beta J(\beta^{(k)})\) and compare to closed form.
  8. Discuss ridge extension \(J_\lambda(\beta)=\frac12\|X\beta-y\|^2+\frac{\lambda}{2}\|\beta\|^2\): changed gradient and solution.

Deliverables in PDF: one-page derivation summary, one numerical convergence experiment, and short interpretation of parameters/residuals.

Takeaway: The gradient unifies local change, directional rates, chain rule structure, and optimization with or without constraints.
