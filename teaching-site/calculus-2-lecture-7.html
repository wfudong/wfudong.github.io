Learning Outcomes

By the end of this lecture, you should be able to:

  • Review the main tools from vectors, geometry, limits, derivatives, extrema, Taylor expansion, and local solvability.
  • Choose the right object for a problem: vector, gradient, Jacobian, Hessian, or Taylor polynomial.
  • Recognize typical pitfalls in limits, parametrizations, boundary extrema, and implicit differentiation.
  • Interpret a double integral over a rectangle as an accumulated total.
  • Set up first iterated integrals and connect determinants/Jacobians to area scaling.

Central Theme

Lectures 1-6 study local structure. Lecture 7 turns that toolkit into a bridge toward accumulated quantities: area, volume, mass, and average value.

Review Map for Lectures 1-6

The review block collects the course vocabulary: dot products and projections, cross products and planes, quadric surfaces, paths for limits, linear approximation, gradients, Jacobians, Hessians, and implicit equations.

A good first question is not "Which formula do I remember?" but "Is this geometry, a limit, a derivative, an optimization problem, or a local solvability problem?"

Representative Problem Types

  • Find a plane through three points using a cross product.
  • Parametrize an intersection curve and check what part of the curve is covered.
  • Disprove a multivariable limit with two paths or a curved path.
  • Combine directional derivatives with tangent planes.
  • Find absolute extrema by comparing interior and boundary candidates.

Comprehensive Problems

The source note includes harder prompts: differentiability counterexamples, determinant differentiation, degenerate critical points, local versus global invertibility, tangent lines to intersection curves, and Lagrange multipliers connected to eigenvalues.

Bridge to Integration

A double integral starts as a Riemann sum over small area elements. On a rectangle, a continuous function can be accumulated by iterated integration:

\[ \iint_R f(x,y)\,dA = \int_a^b\int_c^d f(x,y)\,dy\,dx. \]

The determinant and Jacobian ideas from earlier lectures will soon become the correction factors for coordinate changes.
