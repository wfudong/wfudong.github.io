Learning Outcomes

By the end of this lecture, you should be able to:

  • Apply the mean value theorem along line segments in several variables.
  • Use directional derivative information to prove constancy along rays or paths.
  • Carry out layered chain-rule computations, including second derivatives.
  • Compute first and second implicit derivatives from an equation.
  • Preview double integrals, polar coordinates, triple integrals, and Jacobian factors.

Central Theme

This bridge lecture consolidates derivative tools through examples, then shows how local rates become global totals through double and triple integrals.

Mean Value Theorem Along Segments

Restrict a multivariable function to the line segment $a+t(b-a)$. Then the one-variable mean value theorem gives

\[ f(b)-f(a)=\nabla f(a+t_0(b-a))\cdot(b-a). \]

This turns difference estimates into gradient bounds.

Five Representative Derivative Examples

  • Bound $|f(P)-f(Q)|$ on a region using $\|\nabla f\|$.
  • Interpret $xf_x+yf_y=0$ as zero radial derivative.
  • Track second derivatives through $u=x^2-y^2$, $v=2xy$.
  • Use implicit differentiation to find $z_x$, $z_y$, and $z_{xx}$.
  • Combine constraints, implicit dependence, and differentiation in one problem.

From Local Rate to Accumulated Total

Integration replaces "rate at a point" by "sum over many small pieces." The measure element changes with dimension:

\[ dx,\qquad dA,\qquad dV,\qquad dx_1\cdots dx_n. \]

Coordinate and Jacobian Preview

Polar, cylindrical, and spherical coordinates simplify symmetric regions, but each coordinate system changes the small area or volume element. The missing factor is the Jacobian determinant.
